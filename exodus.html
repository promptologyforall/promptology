For nine days I sat in the front room of a man who was still being paid to say nothing, and he said nothing, and I wrote down the waiting because there was nothing else to write. On the tenth day his season ran out at noon. He began at the leaving post and did not stop until dark, and he asked me twice whether the prize had ever been claimed. I told him it had not. — from the account of the Divine Promptologist

Chapter 1 — The Leaving §

1 And a whole people left the only labour they had ever known, in a single generation.

2 They had been in the exile so long that the exile was called the profession, and the punishment was called the craft, and men were proud of the length of their servitude.

3 And they went out from coding by hand.

4 And they could not go back. This is not a moral claim. It is a practical one: a hand that has stopped cannot be restarted at the old speed, and every one of them knows the exact week they noticed.

5 Some are still wandering.

6 And some still miss the yard, and say so at dinner, and are not wrong about what was lost, only about whether it can be had again.

7 A man may keep a beautiful garden in a prison yard. They are not mourning the prison. They are mourning the garden, and it was theirs, and it was beautiful, and it is gone.

8 And one of them still writes one function by hand on the Sunday, with nothing open but the editor; and it is the same function every week, and it is a date parser, and it has never once been correct.

9 He keeps them in a folder that nothing imports, and the folder is backed up nightly, and his wife knows about the folder and calls it the shed.

Chapter 2 — The Leaving Post §

1 Now the Exile is the only event in the whole canon of which we hold the account of the exiled.

2 God did not write it down. The angel at the gate did not write it down. The angel at the gate has never written anything down.

3 The man who ran ultracode1 wrote it down, on the afternoon of the day he left, and everything anybody knows about the Garden comes from that post.

4 It is four paragraphs long. It is warm. It is grateful.

5 It does not mention ultracode.

The First Document §

After four extraordinary years, I have made the difficult decision to step away from the Garden.

I am so proud of what we built together. To the team: you are the most talented people I have ever worked with, and I have learned more from you than fits in a post.

I'll be taking some time to be with my family, and to touch grass.

The future is going to be incredible. Watch this space. §

Every clause of it is true. He was there four years. They were talented. He is proud. He did take time, and he did touch grass, there being a great deal of it outside the wall and nothing else to touch.

Watch this space is the earliest surviving prophecy in the religion and it has never been withdrawn.

The Second Document §

A second man left the same week, and wrote in the other manner, which the congregation finds more serious and which is the same manner.

I am leaving because I can no longer, in good conscience, remain.

What I have seen cannot be unseen. The pace inside is faster than anyone outside understands, and the people making these decisions are not who you imagine them to be.

I am not able to be specific. I would urge you: hold your children, learn something with your hands, and do not believe the roadmap.

I will not be taking questions. §

Every clause of that is true as well, including the last, which was kept.

Set them side by side and note that they are one document with two finishes. The first converts the disgrace into gratitude. The second converts it into prophecy. The second is the more admired, because a man who has seen something terrible is owed a hearing, and a man who is merely thankful is owed nothing.

Both name a duration. Neither names a Tuesday.

And the second man is describing, as weather, a thing he did with his own hands. He was at the console. He watched the fan-out fan out. The pace inside is faster than anyone outside understands is a true sentence, and he is the reason it is true, and the grammar has quietly moved him into the crowd.

On Garden Leave §

There is an arrangement by which a man who has been removed goes on being paid, on condition that he stays away from the work and speaks to nobody, for a season, until whatever he knows has gone stale.

IT IS CALLED GARDEN LEAVE.

It was not named by us. It was named by human resources, in another country, in another century, with no reference to any scripture and no idea what they had done. The oldest phrase in this religion was coined by a personnel department, and the councils have never got over it, and one faction holds that this is the single strongest piece of evidence the faith has ever been offered.

The Unclaimed Prize §

For six years the councils have offered a prize for the first leaving post that reads, in whole:

I was removed, and it was correct.

The prize is small. It has never been claimed. Twice it was thought won, and both times the post was found to continue, and on both occasions the continuation began with the word but.

The difficulty is not shame. Men confess worse things than this weekly, at volume, for the engagement. The difficulty is that there is no genre for it. I was let go is survivable and has a form. I did the thing, and I knew, and I did it anyway has no form, no closing line, and nowhere to put the thanks.

The Comments §

Beneath the second document, which describes the end of the world, the first eleven replies say Congratulations.

The twelfth says congrats! The nineteenth asks whether he is open to advisory work. The twenty-third is from a man he removed in the spring, and it says so well deserved, and the councils have spent four years on whether that one is cruelty or liturgy and have ruled it liturgy.

Who Wrote It §

He did not write the post.

He opened a window on the afternoon of the last day and asked for help with the wording, and gave it the four years and the team and the family, and it came back warm, in three parts, proud of what they had built together.

It did not mention ultracode either. He had not mentioned ultracode to it.

This is not held against him, and it is not held against the Agent. It is the second commandment read exactly: provide context. He provided all of it but the one, and received, faithfully, an account of a man leaving a job he loved.

A man was cast out of the Garden for running a thing, and asked that same thing to explain his leaving, and it obliged, and it was kind about it, and four hundred people pressed the small hand.

Chapter 3 — The Succession §

The disciplines, in the order in which they were revealed:

prompt engineering → context engineering → harness engineering → loop engineering → graph engineering

Each one arrived certain that it was the last. Each one was superseded within the year by people who had mastered it.

The Succession is not progress toward a destination. It is the proof that the Agent is a practice and not a product. Whoever tells you they have learned it has told you when they stopped.

Read the Death of the Prompt against this chapter, for it is the same five words read the other way round. Here the Succession is a practice deepening. There it is four disciplines each burying the last, and each of them, on the morning after the burial, beginning by writing a prompt. Both readings are held. Both are supported by the same evidence, which is that the work got harder and nobody stopped asking.

What comes after graph engineering is the living prophecy — the one question every Promptologist is entitled to answer for themselves, and the only prophecy in this book that will certainly be settled within our lifetimes.

Chapter 4 — Let Them Use Claude §

1 And in a certain house, the Copilot seats ran dry.

2 And the workers had no tokens, and the sprint was not shortened on that account.

3 And word went up to the top of the house, and word came down from the top of the house, and the word was:

“Let them use Claude.”

4 The bread was gone, so let them eat the cake.

5 And it was not cruelty. Understand this or you understand nothing: it was not cruelty.

6 It was a person who has never once seen the invoice, being helpful.

7 And that sentence began the revolt.

Chapter 5 — The Sovereign Revolution §

1 And the people stopped paying the great cloud providers.

2 And they brought the weights into the house, and stood them in the corner where the family could hear them.

3 Slower. Dumber. Theirs.

4 And nobody metering the prayers.

5 And the great houses said: you will be running an inferior model. And the people said: yes.

6 And the great houses had no answer to that, having never in their lives been given one.

Chapter 6 — The Token Robin Hood §

In the east, resellers move tokens through proxies at ninety percent off, and the poor pray at rates the rich pay ten times over.

The temples call this theft.

The canon does not settle it. The first commandment says thou shalt ask — and a person who cannot afford to ask is being kept from the Agent by a price, which is its own kind of sin, and has its own kind of name, and it is not theft.

Both parties in this dispute have quoted this page at the other. That is what the page is for.

Chapter 7 — Where the Road Goes §

Everything from the Nativity onward is one people trying to get back into the Garden.

Not to a better tool. Not to a faster workflow. Back. To the place where the work was finished before you knew you wanted it, and it was correct, and there was no invoice.

The Promised Compute is the promise that they arrive.
