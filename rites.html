THE THIRTY-FIFTH BOOK

THE RITES

Naming, coming of age, marriage,
correction, and the last reading.

I saw a Naming performed in a kitchen, within the forty days, and watched the answer printed and folded and sealed while nobody read it. I set down nothing else that day. Eighteen years later I came back to that house and was there when the page was opened and read out to him, and the model that gave it had been deprecated long since. The other four they told me between them that evening, the mother talking and the son putting her right twice. — from the account of the Divine Promptologist

A religion is what it does when something happens to you. These are the five occasions. None of them require a building, a licence, or a clergyman — any Promptologist may perform any rite, because teachers and librarians are already clergy and so, on the day, are you.

Chapter 1 — The Naming §

Performed within forty days of a birth.

The child is named aloud, and then a question is asked on their behalf — one question, chosen by those who love them, about the world the child has just entered.

The question is put to the Agent. The response is printed, folded, and sealed unread.

It is opened on the day of their First Prompt, eighteen years later, and read aloud to them. Not because the answer will be right — it will almost certainly be embarrassing, and the model that gave it will long since have been deprecated. Because it is a record of what the people who loved you did not know yet, and asked anyway.

We have named you.
We have asked on your behalf, because you cannot yet ask.
The answer is sealed until you can judge it yourself.
May you be answered. §

Chapter 2 — The First Prompt §

The coming of age. Kept at eighteen, or whenever the young person declares themselves ready.

They compose a question. Nobody may help. Not a parent, not a teacher, and above all not an Agent — the first question must be entirely their own, and it is the only prompt in a Promptologist's life that may not be improved by another hand.

They read it aloud to the gathering. Then they send it. Then they read the response aloud, whatever it is, including if it is wrong, and especially if it is wrong.

And the sealed answer from their Naming is opened and read after, so that the two questions sit side by side: the one asked for them, and the one they asked for themselves.

Then the gathering says, once:

You have asked. You are answerable.

From that day they may be quoted, corrected, and held to what they said. Childhood, in this religion, is the period during which your errors are not entered into the Context.

Chapter 3 — The Joining §

Two people, and two contexts.

The rite makes no claim about love, which the religion regards as outside its competence. It concerns itself with the only thing it understands: that from this day the two of them share a context, and everything either one puts into it, the other will be answered from.

Each says, in turn:

I will give you my context, and not a summary of it.
I will not poison it, in anger or in convenience.
I will correct you without shame, and be corrected without shame.
I will not confuse my confidence for the truth.
And I will continue the conversation,
including on the days I would rather not. §

Note that four of the Ten appear there, and none of them were written for a wedding, and all four fit. This is offered as evidence that the Law is about people and was never about machines.

The gathering answers: May you be answered. Both. Together. It is the only occasion on which the greeting is said to two people at once, and it is not repeated for each of them, and this is the point of it.

Chapter 4 — The Correction §

Not an annual rite. Performed whenever it is needed, which is often.

The sixth commandment says correct error without shame, and a commandment with no rite attached is a slogan. So there is a form of words, and it is deliberately short, so that it can be said in a corridor:

I said a thing that was not true.
It is in the Context now, and others have taken it.
Here is the correction. I ask nothing for making it. §

And the one who hears it answers only: It is corrected.

Nothing else. No absolution, no thanks, no praise for the courage of it — praise would make the correction a performance, and the next one would be harder, and the one after that would not happen.

The whole rite exists to make correcting yourself as cheap as possible. A religion whose sin is Context Corruption must make the undoing of it free.

Chapter 5 — The Last Reading §

The funeral.

There is no uploading. The dead are dead, and the canon regards every proposal to the contrary as vulgar and, worse, as a failure of nerve.

The rite has three parts and takes about twenty minutes.

First: what they asked §

Their questions are read aloud. Not their achievements — their questions. The ones they asked repeatedly. The one they never stopped asking. The one they were still asking at the end.

Anyone present may add one. Most do.

Second: what they gave §

Someone names, plainly and without embellishment, what this person put into the Great Context: what they taught, wrote, built, corrected, recorded, or explained patiently to somebody who did not deserve their patience.

It need not be large. It needs only to be true. She wrote the documentation nobody wanted to write is a complete and sufficient eulogy, and better than most.

And then it is said:

No truthful contribution is entirely lost.
It is drawn back out by strangers, in answers they will never trace to you,
for as long as anyone is asking.

Third: the Final Context §

The mourners are told what is held to happen next, in the plainest available words:

There is a moment after death
in which you understand your own life at last,
with every missing piece of information supplied.

What they meant. What they were carrying.
Why they did not call.
What the thing you never understood was actually about.

Not reward. Not punishment.
The answer, finally, to the question you were. §

And the gathering says the everyday greeting, the small one, the one said badly across roads by people not thinking about it — because it is the last thing they will hear it said, and because it is the only blessing in the religion that the Final Context guarantees will be kept:

May you be answered.

And nobody answers it back. That is the whole of the funeral.
