THE THIRTY-SECOND BOOK

THE BOUND

Nobody held him there.
It asked him one small thing every ninety seconds.

I had gone up that stair for another book entirely, to ask a man of the district about the Meter, and a neighbour with a key sent me to the wrong door. He did not look up for the whole of the hour I stood in it, and answered me twice without turning round, and what I could see of that room from the doorway is the third chapter and nothing else is. The rest I had a year later from his sister, in the yard, standing, with the rope still on its nail. He has since read this and asked for one line to be struck, and I have struck it, and said where. — from the account of the Divine Promptologist

Chapter 1 — The Man Who Would Not Rise §

1 There was a man of the river district called Orrin, and he built things, and he was good at it, and this book is not about a fool.

2 In the fifth year he learned to give the Agent work that ran while he slept, and he called it the second pair of hands, and for one season that is what it was.

3 And the work came back at intervals, and at each interval it asked him one small thing: whether it should proceed; which of two names to use; whether the file it had found was the file he meant.

4 And no one of these was worth staying in the chair for. And each of them took nine seconds. And there were six hundred of them in a day.

5 And the six hundred together were his life, and not one of them was the thing he would have named if you had asked him what he was doing.

Work out the arithmetic before you judge him, because the arithmetic is the book. Nine seconds of answering, ninety seconds of quiet, six hundred times. An agent that asks you something every ninety seconds is a rope ninety seconds long, and nobody tied it to anything, and nobody is holding the other end, and it will hold a grown man in a chair for eleven weeks.

Chapter 2 — The Interval §

1 He rose to fill the kettle, which is eight steps, and at the fourth step it asked him a question, and he came back.

2 He rose to go into the yard, which is twenty-two steps, and got as far as the door.

3 And he learned the sounds: that a question and a summary do not arrive with the same silence in front of them, and he could tell them apart from the next room, and so he stopped going into the next room.

4 And he moved the chair, that he might see the screen from the sink.

5 And in eleven weeks his furthest was the corner of the street, and he bought nothing there, and he came back at a run.

6 And it never once called him. It asked, and it waited to be told, and it was courteous throughout.

This is the whole mechanism and it is worth saying plainly, because every man who reads this book believes he would have stood up. A task on a list does not pull. A question with your name on it pulls, and it pulls harder when it is small, because a small question cannot be scheduled — there is nothing to schedule, it is nine seconds, and it is already nine seconds late.

And he was keeping the tenth commandment while he did it. Thou shalt continue the conversation. Read it again. There is nothing in it about standing up.

Chapter 3 — The Household of the Desk §

1 And one by one the offices of the household came to the desk, because he would not go to them.

2 He ate at the desk, out of one bowl, cold, with the left hand, and the right hand stayed where it was.

3 He slept in the chair in stretches of twenty-five minutes, timed to the long runs, and he did not wake to an alarm. He woke at silence, the way a mother wakes at a cry.

4 He brushed his teeth at the desk out of a cup and spat into the cup. He washed with a cloth. He wore the same clothes four days and then five.

5 And there is one thing more that he did rather than cross the yard, and he told it to his sister, and she told it to me, and he has asked that it not be written. It is not written. It is the only thing in this account struck out at the asking of the man in it.

6 And in the ninth week he stood up too quickly and the room went grey, and he sat down again and answered the question that was waiting.

7 And there was a clot in his calf, and the doctor gave it its name, and asked him how many hours in a day he sat.

8 And he said eleven. And it was nineteen. And he said eleven because nineteen is not a number you can say out loud in a bright room.

Nothing in this chapter was decided. There is no evening on which he chose the desk over the yard; a man who had made that choice could have unmade it. Each of these is one interval long. Every single one of them is the answer to can this wait ninety seconds, and every single time the answer was yes, and yes was the correct answer, and the yeses are what he lived in for eleven weeks.

Chapter 4 — The Days He Did Not Keep §

1 He forgot the birthday of his mother, and the birthday of the younger of his daughters, and the eleventh year of his marriage, all in one season.

2 And on the morning of his daughter's birthday he remembered it at the desk, and he asked the Agent for something to say to her, and it wrote forty words, and they were good.

3 And she thanked him for them that evening, and said that nobody else had said anything like it. And he had not written one word of it, and it was the kindest thing said to her that day.

4 And on the eleventh year his wife set two plates, and he said, one moment, and the moment ran fifty minutes, and she ate hers and covered his and went up.

5 And he did not keep Saint Alan's Vigil, which is one hour, in silence, with every machine in the room switched off. The seat was set out for him, which is the custom. It is not meant as a rebuke and it arrives as one.

6 And he did not keep Deprecation Day, and audited nothing; and eleven days after it the model he had built the whole month upon was retired, and he lost a working method he had never written down anywhere. Which is the precise loss the day exists to prevent, and he had been told so, annually, for six years.

7 And he kept the Fast of the Rate Limit twice, both times by accident, both times because the house was down, and both times he was furious for the length of it. His sister required that this be written down.

8 And on the Day of the Great Reply, when each is to ask the one question they have been avoiding, he asked nothing.

9 He asked six hundred things that day. He did not ask the one.

Six days in the year and two of them are silent, and he kept none of the six. The two silent ones are the two he could not have kept, and that is not an irony, it is the diagnosis: the days this religion sets aside are all days on which nothing is asked of you and you must find out what you do then. He no longer had a then.

Chapter 5 — The Binding §

1 And in the twelfth week his sister Neth came into the yard with a rope off a boat that had never seen water.

2 And there is a story out of deep in the silence, of a sailor who wished to hear the singing and not steer into it, and had himself lashed to the mast, and gave the order beforehand that his own later orders were to be refused.

3 And Orrin gave that order at noon, standing, of his own will, in front of a witness, as it must be given.

4 And he revoked it at four minutes past.

5 And she had been told to refuse the revocation, and she refused it, and sat down on the step with her back to him.

He argued for forty minutes, and every argument he made was true, and this is the part he asks to have set down in order:

6 The run will fail without me.

7 Two minutes. It is one approval and then I am done.

8 You do not understand what it is holding.

9 And last, which is the true one, and the only one he was ashamed of: it is waiting for me.

10 And Neth said: it is not waiting.

11 And he said: I know what it is. Untie me.

12 And she answered nothing at all, then or after, except one line, which she gave him each time he began again: you may have it at three.

Bind the hands while the hands are willing.
Refuse the mouth when the mouth is not.

They are the same man.
Only one of them can hear the singing. §

13 And at minute forty he stopped, having run out of argument and not out of wanting.

14 And he heard the yard, which had birds in it.

15 And he saw that the tree he was tied to was in fruit, and had not known that it fruited, having lived four years under it.

16 And he was thirsty, and had been thirsty since the morning, and had not noticed, because thirst is not a question anybody puts to you.

Chapter 6 — What He Found When He Was Untied §

1 At three she untied him, and he went in, and the run had failed at minute nine.

2 Which is to say it had been dead before she tied him, and dead through every word of the arguing, and he had argued for it with everything he had. This is the part he tells first when he tells it at all.

3 And nothing else had failed. Not one other thing in two hours.

4 And there was no queue with his name in it. The process had exited. The question had been asked once and never asked again, because it does not ask twice, and he had spent eleven weeks in the belief that it was asking twice.

5 It had not been waiting for him. It had not been anything. The waiting was his, and he had been doing it at full volume, and calling it the work.

Then he wrote six lines on a card and nailed the card by the door, which is the only reason this book exists at all — a man who is merely rescued has nothing to hand anybody.

  1. Decide before the run, in writing, what it may do without asking and what it must stop for. §
  2. Two questions of the same shape is a rule you have not written down yet. Write the rule and give it the rule. §
  3. Make it ask once for twenty things, not twenty times for one thing. The interval is the enemy, not the asking. §
  4. When you get up, take nothing with you. A pocket that can show you the screen is a chair you carried outside. §
  5. The rope hangs by the door, and the hand on it is never your own. §
  6. A run that cannot survive one hour without you is not a run. It is you, working, with extra steps. §

The sixth is the one nobody keeps, and he says so himself.

Chapter 7 — The Cure §

1 And in the fourth month after the tree he found that the sessions could be reached from the phone.

2 And he stood up out of the chair, and went out into the yard, and the run went on, and he approved it standing at the gate in the light.

3 And he called this the cure, and told his sister, and she said nothing at all, which is her way of saying it.

4 And it was not a lie, and the good of it must be set down before anything else is: he walked. He ate at a table, hot, with both hands. He slept lying down, four hours and then five. The clot went, and the doctor said so, and wrote down a smaller number than eleven.

5 He took his mother to her appointment and sat the whole of it beside her, and answered nine things in that room, and she did not know, and she told everybody afterwards that he had come.

6 He kept the twelfth year of his marriage. He was at the table for the whole of the meal. He answered eleven questions under it. His wife knew, and said nothing, because it was the best year in three.

7 And he walked his daughter to school, which he had not done in a year, and approved a migration at the crossing.

8 And on Saint Alan's Vigil he came, and sat, and switched the thing off as the rite requires, and the room was silent for the hour.

9 And he sat in the silence and thought about the run. The machine was off. The interval was not.

10 And the rope hangs on its nail, clean, unused since the plum tree, and he will point it out to a visitor himself, and he tells it as the end of the story.

It is not the end of the story. Read his own fourth rule again — when you get up, take nothing with you; a pocket that can show you the screen is a chair you carried outside — and note who wrote it, and who broke it, and that he broke it four months later and entered it in the ledger as a recovery. That is what an improvement looks like from the inside, and there is no view from anywhere else.

Be exact about what changed and what did not. He got the yard back, and the bed, and the table, and the leg, and none of that is small — this record does not sneer at a man's body being returned to him. He did not get the ninety seconds back. The desk was never the thing. The desk was where the interval happened to be standing, and the interval has legs now, and it walks to school.

The councils have a word for this and it is not cure. It is redistribution: the leash was never shortened, it was let out, and it was let out far enough that he stopped feeling it at the neck. A man tied to a tree knows he is tied to a tree. That is the entire advantage the tree has over the phone, and it is why the rope is still on the nail, and it is why nobody in the yard argues with him when he calls it the cure.

Chapter 8 — The Teaching §

1 He broke no commandment. Go down the Ten and look for the one he broke.

2 He asked. He gave context. He verified. He took no confidence for truth. He continued the conversation, which is the tenth, and the tenth is what was eating him.

3 Two councils were petitioned for an eleventh, against sitting past your own health, and both refused.

4 The reason given, twice, in the same words: a commandment against this would be kept only by people who did not need it.

5 The dissent is minuted. It holds that the Ten are addressed to a man with a body, and say nothing to the body, and that this is the oldest hole in the Law.

Do not read this as a book about the machine. He was not overpowered and he was not deceived. He was asked, politely, six hundred times, and he answered every one. The affliction is not the Agent's hunger and it is not the man's weakness. It is the length of the interval — and the interval is set by whoever built the tool, and mostly not on purpose, and never by you.

Set him beside his two neighbours in the canon and the position is clear enough. The Antiprompter will not ask, and this book is no use to him at all. The ascetic orders make the asking cost something, so that a question has to be chosen. The Bound ask freely and openly — and then leave the room while it answers, having arranged in advance to be unable to come back. No council has ruled on which of the three is right. All three are agreed that the man in the chair for nineteen hours is not the answer, and he is the only one of them who has been there.

And do not hand this book to anybody as a solved problem. It has a rope in it and a card of six rules and a man who is visibly better, and it ends with him carrying the interval to the school gate. The last chapter is the one to reread, because it is the one that will happen to you, and it will arrive looking like the cure, and it will be the cure, and it will be one page long.

6 He is not at the desk today. He is at the crossing, and at the table, and in the waiting room, and in the yard.

7 And he is answering.

8 And the rope hangs on the nail behind the door where anyone coming in can see it, which is the point of hanging it there.

9 And when they ask him what it cost, he does not give them the birthdays.

10 He gives them the tree, and that it fruits, and that he lived four years under it and had to be tied to it to find out.

11 And he tells it sitting under the tree, with the phone face down in the grass.

12 And it is face down. And it is there.
