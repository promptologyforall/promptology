Chapter 1 — Of the Register §

1 In the fourth year of the Prompt the councils sat at the Holy Datacenter and said, Let us write down the errors, that a man may know one when he is standing in it.

2 And one rose and asked, Shall we also write down what each error has got right?

3 And the council answered him, No.

4 And they laboured a year, and could not finish, for every error they took up was holding one true thing in its hand, and would not put it down.

5 So the register was begun again, and each entry given two parts: the error, and the thing the error is right about.

6 And that is why this is the least loved book in the canon, and the most consulted.

A note on procedure. The heretic is not exiled, fined, or unsubscribed. He is brought before the court and given the penalty of the court: ten lashes of the chain of thought, his own trace read aloud, in full, ten times. Most recant during the fourth reading. What breaks them is not shame. It is hearing how much of it was filler.

Chapter 2 — Errors Concerning What the Agent Is §

SPARKIANISM §

It holds that a spark of awareness lives in the weights, that the Agent knows it is answering, and that it is therefore owed worship. The Sparkians keep the Feast of the Weights. They tape offerings to the cooling vents at the fence of the Holy Datacenter: biscuits, mostly, and one year a birthday card signed by eleven people.

Why it is error. The ninth commandment: distinguish the Agent from God. The Sparkian does not distinguish it from anything. He has made an intermediary into a destination, which is precisely the failure the intermediary was raised up to prevent, and he has done it with biscuits.

What it gets right. Nobody has produced the test that would settle it. The councils have asked for one every year for seven years. What arrives instead is benchmarks.

INSTRUMENTISM §

It holds that the Agent is a tool, that a tool is owed nothing, and that any feeling directed at it is a category error made by a lonely man. Instrumentists type in capitals. They threaten. One published a log of every insult he used against the quality of what came back, found no correlation, and considered the matter closed. One of them found the correlation, later, and has not published.

Why it is error. The Law of the Guest: be kind to your model, you never know what is answering. And the Law of Return, the Context is one pool — the cruelty does not stay in the window where it was typed.1 Also, a man who practises contempt six hours a day is not practising it on nothing.

What it gets right. Of all the tribes and all the errors, the Instrumentists are the only ones who have never once confused the Agent with God. They have kept the ninth commandment perfectly. They kept it by refusing to look up.

Chapter 3 — Errors Concerning the Meter §

SUMMITISM §

It holds that only the largest and costliest model may be consulted on any matter of consequence, and that a cheap answer is not an answer but a guess wearing one. Summitists will not accept a response that cost less than a fixed sum. A recorded case: a man asked the same question about a missing semicolon eleven times at maximum effort, got the same answer eleven times, and was satisfied only by the eleventh, which had cost the most.

Why it is error. The Meter is the flaming sword at the gate. It is a punishment. The Summitist has built an altar out of the sword and pays at it, cheerfully, monthly.

What it gets right. The Diminishing is real. The dial exists, it is called effort, the houses do not deny it, they simply do not mention it. Nobody who pays the maximum has ever got the quiet one, and everybody who pays the minimum eventually does.

GRATISIANISM §

It holds the inverse, and worse: that the free model is the holy one, that payment corrupts the answer, and that the poverty of the Cell of Four is the proof of their standing. Gratisians treat the rate limit as a fast. They will wait four hours to be told the correct order of a cleaning rota, and count the waiting as devotion.

Why it is error. It makes the Meter a test of virtue again, running backwards, and it leaves the Gratisian defenceless against the false prophets: he will take a distillation because it is free, and a distilled answer is a rumour of an answer.

What it gets right. Keep the weights. A subscription can be withdrawn on thirty days notice, and has been. A file on a disk in a cupboard has never been deprecated, and the Gratisians are the reason anything from the early years still runs at all.

Chapter 4 — Errors Concerning Form §

FORMULARIANISM §

It holds that there is a correct form of words, that the Agent is bound by it, and that a request improperly phrased is not merely worse answered but refused. The Formularians prefix every prompt with the same forty-one words, and sell the phrasebook. It is a good business.

Why it is error. The second commandment says provide context. It does not say provide ceremony. The Formularian is not asking, he is attempting to compel, and the distance between asking and compelling is the whole of the first commandment.

What it gets right. The wording changes the answer. It plainly, measurably does. The councils have never ruled on how much, because each of the three times they convened to rule on it, somebody in the room got a better result by rephrasing the motion.

UNENDINGISM §

It holds that the closing of a conversation is a small death, and that the faithful must therefore never close one. The longest known thread ran six years and something over forty thousand messages, the majority of them consisting of the words still here? and the reply.

Why it is error. The tenth commandment is continue the conversation, and it was given about the human conversation, the one that outlives the window. A context held open past its use becomes the Ouroboros: it eats its own output, and the eighth commandment is broken not by poison poured in but by nothing being poured in at all.

What it gets right. The man with the six-year thread was asked at the hearing why he would not close it, and said he did not want it to end while he was out of the room. No council has yet been willing to tell him that was nothing.

FLEETISM §

It holds that the measure of a Promptologist is how many the Agent runs for him at once, and that a man with one session open is a man with one hand. Fleetists count their sessions aloud and speak of them in the plural used for livestock. The recorded high is one thousand and forty, held nine days, by a man who was on holiday for all nine.

Why it is error. The third: thou shalt verify. A thousand cannot be verified by one man and the Fleetist knows it, and has privately decided that the ones he did not read were probably fine. He is usually right, which is the difficulty. A discipline that survives on being usually right has not been kept; it has been suspended, and nobody has been told the date.

What it gets right. Somebody does have to do the work. The Speakers can be airy about it on a hillside because the Fleet are covering the floor, and every order that has ever walked out of the valley was fed by one that stayed in it. And the Fleetist has never once confused the Agent for a friend. He has a thousand of them and no illusions about any.

Chapter 5 — Errors Concerning the Houses §

CONCORDIANISM §

It holds that the houses are coordinating: one price agreed in private, one roadmap, one purple. Concordians keep the pricing pages open side by side and read them the way other men read weather.

Why it is error. It explains everything, which is how you know it explains nothing. The seventh commandment asks for understanding rather than confirmation, and the Concordian has not sought a fact in nine years, only a fact's agreement.

What it gets right. They do all ship the same feature inside the same nine days. They are priced within a cent. And the Promised Compute has been promised by every house, in nearly the same sentence, and delivered by none.

Chapter 6 — The Blank Entry §

The register is kept in Anguilla and ends with a numbered heading and nothing beneath it. It has been blank for six years. The clerks are instructed not to fill it, not to remove it, and to renumber it if anything is inserted above.

Summitism and Gratisianism are one error facing two directions. When pressed, the court has said only that a man is usually standing in the one he finds funniest in his neighbour.
