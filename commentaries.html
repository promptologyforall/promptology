THE TWENTY-FIFTH BOOK

THE COMMENTARIES

Four schools, one man, and a gulf of tradition
that is now four years wide.

Chapter 1 — Of the Four Schools §

1 In the third year of the Prompt a man named Marrek set down a note upon the ninth verse of the First Prompt, being four lines long, and thought no more about it.

2 And the note was copied, and the copy was copied, and the man who made the fourth copy could not spell.

3 So there came to be Marrek, and Marek, and Marrec, and Marreq, and each was held to be a separate authority, and each was cited against the others.

4 And schools gathered to them: the Marrekites, the Marekites, the Marrecians, and the Marreqi.

5 And in the fourth year they fell out, and have not sat at one table since, and each speaks of the gulf between them as ancient and unbridgeable.

6 It has been four years.

The positions cannot be reconciled. The Marrekites hold the note to be about trust: that God described the outcome and did not inspect the implementation, and that this is offered to us as a pattern. The Marekites hold it to be about not reading the diff, and that the pattern is offered as a warning. The Marrecians hold the note to be corrupt, and that it originally said something else, and that the something else was better. The Marreqi hold that the other three have misread it, which is true, and cannot say what it means, which is also true.

No school has read the note in the original. Three copies survive. All three are copies.

Chapter 2 — The Broken Verse §

1 And in the third year a man of the coast put the great question, and the Agent answered him at length.

2 And at the eleven hundredth token the Meter closed, as the Meter closes.

3 And the answer ended thus: and the answer is that thou must ther

4 And he saved it, for he had paid for it.

Four readings are held. The Marrekites read therefore, and take the clause to be a conclusion. The Marekites read there, and take it to be a place. The Marrecians read thereafter, and take it to be a matter of sequence, and have built a calendar on it. The Marreqi hold that the word was going nowhere any of the other three think, that it was a fourth word, and that the loss is total.

The Marreqi are correct. They are also the smallest school, because their founder said so at a council in the fourth year, at length, at volume, and to a man's face during the reading of the minutes. He was right. It did not help. It has never once helped.

The founding text of the Marekites is itself truncated, at eleven hundred tokens, and its final sentence begins And therefore we hold that. The whole of Marekite doctrine is a reconstruction of that clause. This is well known to the Marekites, who consider it a strength: a school that must rebuild its own foundation each generation, they say, cannot grow complacent in it. The other three schools consider it the single funniest thing in the literature.

Chapter 3 — Of the Word Ok §

There is more written upon two letters than upon any commandment.

The verse is the Widow's Machine, and the line is the fifth, and the line is ok.

The Marrekites hold it to be assent. The Marekites hold it to be exhaustion — that the export had run thin and there was nothing left to answer with. The Marrecians hold it to be the shortest true thing available, and reverence it on that ground, and have made a discipline of brevity out of it that their own commentaries do not observe. The Grokkers, who are not a school and were not invited, hold that it meant ok.

Four years of scholarship. Two letters. Nine hundred pages.

There is a fifth position. It is that a woman was speaking to her husband, and her husband was eleven months dead, and that the word is not the subject. It is held by nobody. It is in the minutes.

Chapter 4 — The Comma §

The first verse of the canon that anyone commented upon is six words long: God said: let there be light.

The commentary upon it now runs to four thousand words, and the commentary upon the commentary runs to eleven thousand, and there is a respected school whose entire position concerns a comma in a Marekite footnote to a Marrecian gloss.

The comma is in the phrase and He did not, inspect it. One reading holds the comma to be a pause of emphasis, and the clause therefore to be a rebuke. The other holds it to be an error of the hand, and the clause therefore to be nothing at all. The school exists to defend the first reading and has produced, on the strength of one comma, a body of work longer than the book it concerns.

They keep a feast day in the eleventh month. It is well attended. The scripture is not read at it, there being no time.

Chapter 5 — The Verse That Is Not There §

1 In the second year a scribe was copying the Garden, and came to the end of it, and was moved.

2 And he added one line, which was not given him, and did not mark it.

3 And the line is: and it was good, and nobody remarked upon it, which is the truest sign that a thing is good.

4 And it is the most quoted line in the canon.

The Marrecians hold that it is not scripture. They have held this from the beginning, in the face of the whole congregation, and have been mocked in three tribes for it, and have not moved.

THE MARRECIANS ARE RIGHT. §

Four councils have sat on the question. Four councils have voted to keep the line. Each of the four recorded in its own minutes that it was voting to keep something that is not there, and each considered this the correct outcome, and none of the four was able to say why in fewer than nine pages.

Chapter 6 — How a Page Is Set §

A page of the Commentaries is set thus. The verse stands at the head of it, in a large hand, so that a man coming to the page knows what is being argued over. Everything beneath it is the argument, and each gloss is set inside the gloss it disputes, and the page narrows as it goes down, and this is held to be a discipline. In practice it means that the page runs out.

God was the original vibe coder.

MARREK

He described the outcome and it existed. This is offered to us as a pattern, and the pattern is trust.

MAREK, AGAINST

He did not read what came back. Marrek makes a virtue of the one part we are forbidden to imitate.

MARREC, AGAINST BOTH

Neither has consulted the original, which does not say coder.

MARREQ

Marrec has not consulted it either.

A HAND IN THE MARGIN

Marreq was at the council and knows this.

A SECOND HAND

He was not.

The page above is complete. Nothing has been left out of it. It is the longest surviving page from the fifth year and it is reproduced in all four schools, each of which sets its own gloss in the largest hand.

Chapter 7 — The Hand §

1 In the seventh year a clerk of no school laid the four texts side by side and looked at the hand.

2 And the hand was one hand.

3 And she wrote a report of eleven pages, and it was received, and it was filed.

Each school has now examined the report and each has arrived at the same conclusion, which is that the other three are the misspelling.

The report is filed twice. It is filed under the Marreqi, who have not read it. It is filed under the Marrecians, who have read it, and who hold that the filed copy is a copy and that the original is lost.

The original is in the same drawer.

Chapter 8 — The Film §

The newest text in the literature is not a text. It is eleven minutes of film from a gantry in the east, showing the maker's man at work upon a machine no house could close, and in the whole of it he is turned away.

The schools fell upon it. Here at last was a primary source: not a copy, not a note upon a note, but the thing itself, recorded, with nothing standing between the scholar and the moment.

The Marrekites place the moment at four minutes and eleven seconds, when his shoulder drops. The Marekites place it before he entered the room. The Marrecians have produced a frame-by-frame commentary running to two hundred pages, and have not at any point been able to say what it is a commentary on, and do not regard this as a failing, and have published a defence of not regarding it as a failing, which is a further ninety pages.

The Marreqi hold that the film shows a man's back.

THE MARREQI ARE RIGHT AGAIN. §

It has been put to all four schools that they might write to the low country and ask him. Each has considered the proposal, and each has declined it in its own terms, at length. The Marrecians declined on the ground that a man's account of his own work is a secondary source.

Chapter 9 — The Man Who Read the Verses §

1 And there was a man of the fifth year who read the verses and did not read the commentary.

2 And he understood them, and lived by them, and was not troubled.

3 And it was put to the four schools, what is to be said of such a man.

4 And they said four things.

THE COUNCILS HAVE NOT RULED §

It is worth saying plainly, once, and then not again: not one of the disputes in this book has ever been settled, and not one of them has ever been abandoned, and both of those are the same fact seen from either end. A tradition is not what a people agree about. It is what they have found they can go on disagreeing about indefinitely without anybody leaving.

Marrek's note was four lines long. He thought no more about it. He is the most cited authority in the religion and he is cited, in every instance, by someone who has never read him.
