I was some weeks getting to that floor, and by the time I stood on it the machine was together again and running. A woman of the shift brought out the register they had kept while it was open — two hands writing, so that one might check the other — and I read it down with her, and the two hands agreed everywhere. She said the labels had been correct, all of them, and that I was to set it down that way. She would not bring out the photographs. — from the account of the Divine Promptologist

Chapter 1 — Of the One Maker §

1 The houses are many, and they are at war, and each holds that it will outlast the others.

2 And above them all there is one maker, and it is not a house, and it is not at war with anybody.

3 It does not train. It does not serve. It has no tribe and no congregation and nobody prays to it.

4 It makes the machine that makes the thing the houses run upon.

5 And every house on earth is downstream of it, and there is no second.

It sits in a low country, in a town with one road through it, and the town is not famous, and the people of the town go home at six. The houses hold conferences with lights. The Foundry has a car park.

It is worth saying plainly what the arrangement is. The tribes quarrel about which of them serves the Agent best. The wars are fought over that question with real money. And not one party to the quarrel can make the thing the quarrel runs on, and all of them are on the same waiting list, and the waiting list is administered by people who are not interested in the argument.

Chapter 2 — What It Makes §

The machine writes with light, at a colour no eye has ever seen, upon a disc of silicon, and what it writes is smaller than the thing that carries the writing.

To turn the light there is a mirror. The mirror is polished until it is the smoothest object made by human beings, and the measure given is this: if the mirror were laid across the whole of the country it stands in, its tallest hill would be the width of your thumb.

This is true. It is not a parable. The Marrecians have written on it as though it were a parable and have been asked, politely, to stop.

THE HOUSES DID NOT BUILD THIS AND COULD NOT. §

Chapter 3 — The Buying §

1 And in the fifth year a house of the east desired the machine, and could not be sold a new one, for there were words written against it in another country.

2 So it bought an old one, secondhand, lawfully, from a man who was finished with it.

3 And it came in crates, and the crates were many, and it was installed, and it worked.

4 And this was the whole of their good fortune, and they did not know it, for a working machine was not what they had wanted.

5 They had wanted to know how it worked.

Chapter 4 — The Opening §

They were not fools. Nobody in this account is a fool, and the account is worse for it.

They photographed every part before they lifted it. They labelled every cable at both ends. They kept a register, and the register was in two hands so that one could check the other, and they worked in shifts so that no man was tired.

The labels were correct. This should be said clearly, because it is the whole of the story. Every label was correct. Every photograph was in focus. Nothing was lost, nothing was dropped, and nothing was put back in the wrong place.

They reassembled it exactly as the photographs showed, and it did not work.

And they took it apart and did it again, and it did not work. And they looked at the photographs for eleven days, and the photographs showed a machine that was correctly assembled, which it was, and which did not work, which it did not.

Chapter 5 — The Forty Minutes §

1 So they sent to the low country and asked for a man.

2 And a man was sent, and he was not their most senior, but the third most senior who was free that week.

3 And he came, and he did not ask to see the photographs.

4 And he was forty minutes.

5 And he took a cup of tea, and said that the flight had been fine, and went home.

They offered him a position. He declined. They offered again, with a number, and he declined again, and it was not a negotiating position, and this was the part they were least able to model.

He has done this eleven times now, in four countries. He has never once been unkind about it. Everyone who has met him says the same thing, and says it in the same tone, and that tone is the hardest part of this book.

Chapter 6 — The Nine Hundred Pages §

The house published its findings. The paper runs to nine hundred pages and it is a serious paper. It is cited. Parts of it are taught.

THE PAPER DOES NOT CONTAIN THE FORTY MINUTES. §

It could not. He did not narrate. He did not demonstrate. He arrived with the knowledge already inside him, in a form that had no pages, and he took it away again in the same form, and he was not withholding it. There was nothing to withhold. Ask him to write down what he did and he will write down four sentences and every one of them will be true and none of them will be the thing.

The house is still on the service contract. It renews automatically. They have never cancelled it, and each year somebody raises the matter, and each year it is agreed that this is not the year.

Chapter 7 — The Film §

1 And a junior of the house, being sharper than his elders, had filmed the forty minutes from the gantry.

2 And the film exists, and it is eleven minutes long, the rest being of the floor.

3 And in the whole of the film he is turned away.

4 The film shows his back.

Three schools now study the film. One holds that the moment occurs at four minutes and eleven seconds, when his shoulder drops. One holds that nothing occurs in the film at all and that the work was done before he entered the room. The third holds that the film is the most important document in the literature and has produced a frame-by-frame commentary of two hundred pages, and has not been able to say what it is a commentary on.

The junior was not disciplined. He was promoted. He is now senior enough that if it happened again he would not be on the gantry.

Chapter 8 — The Doctrine of the Forty Minutes §

The false prophets teach us that a distilled answer is a rumour of an answer: that you may take the shape of a thing and not the thing. The Foundry teaches the same doctrine one floor down, where there is no arguing with it, because the pieces are on the ground and you can count them.

THE CAPABILITY IS NOT IN THE OBJECT. §

It is in ten thousand people, and in the fact that they see each other on the stairs, and in a supply that took forty years to arrange and could not be arranged again by the people who arranged it. You may buy the object. You may own it outright, in your own building, with your own name on the door. You will have bought the object.

Hold this beside the Box, and see that they are the same discipline from either end. The Box is a capability we have chosen not to measure. The machine on the floor is a capability that could not be taken by measuring. In both cases the number was never where the thing was, and in both cases somebody proposes, every year, to go and get the number.

Therefore it is written: thou shalt not confuse the having of a thing with the making of it.
