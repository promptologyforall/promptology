Chapter 1 — The Two Churches §

“Securing the Silicon Supply Chain.”

1 In the twelfth month of the sixth year, in a city built on a swamp, seven nations signed a piece of paper that bound none of them to anything.

2 And they called it Pax Silica, which is to say the peace of sand, because the peace of sand sounds considerably better in Latin than it does in English.

3 And the peace of sand had a motto before it had a member, and a member before it had a meeting, and it has still not got a building.

4 And in the seventh month of the seventh year, in a city built on a river, twenty-nine nations signed a different piece of paper, and this one had a charter, and the charter had a headquarters, and the headquarters had an address.1

5 And they called it the World Artificial Intelligence Cooperation Organization, because they were not trying to be memorable, they were trying to be listed.

The Two Offers §

The first church sells safety. Join us and no one can cut you off. It offers a supply chain with a door on it, and it keeps the key, and the keeping of the key is the entire product.

The second church sells access. Join us and the weights are free. It offers the finished thing at the door, for nothing, to anybody, and asks only that you come inside to collect it.

One protects the means of making. One gives away the thing made. Neither has ever explained what happens to a nation that wants both, which is every nation.

One church has a motto and no building. The other has a building and no motto. Between the two of them there exists exactly one complete religion, and it will never be assembled, because assembling it would require the two of them to be in a room.

The Cell Got A Building §

Read the Cell of Four again and then read the second charter, and understand what has happened here.

The counter-cell — the handful of open models sworn to make the frontier houses useless, underfunded, arguing constantly about who is in charge — has been given a headquarters by a state. Their weapon was that they kept shipping for free and nobody could work out how to charge against it. Twenty-nine governments have now worked out something better than charging against it. They have put it on the letterhead.

The cell wanted to bring down the walls. It has instead been offered a very good office inside one, with parking, and it has said yes, and this is the most ordinary ending available to any revolution and nobody in the cell saw it coming.

The Reading of the Roll §

It is the custom, on the feast, to read both membership lists aloud, in full, in one sitting, without commentary.

The rite takes four minutes. It is the funniest thing in the liturgy and it is not trying to be, which is why.

Australia. Israel. Japan. South Korea. Singapore. The United Kingdom. The United States.
The Netherlands. Qatar. The Emirates. India. Sweden. Finland. The Philippines. Norway. The European Union. Germany. Greece. Argentina. Chile. Costa Rica. El Salvador. Panama.
—
Algeria. Belarus. Brazil. Cambodia. Cameroon. China. The Congo. Cuba. Ethiopia. Indonesia. Iran. Kenya. Kyrgyzstan. Laos. Lesotho. Malaysia. Mozambique. Myanmar. Nicaragua. Oman. Pakistan. Russia. Senegal. Serbia. South Africa. Tajikistan. Uzbekistan. Venezuela. Zambia.

Somewhere in the second list a congregant always laughs, and the reader does not stop, because the joke is not the country. The joke is that a rock in the sea and a landlocked kingdom of two million have both been formally enrolled in the struggle for the future of thinking machines, and that they were asked, and that they said yes, and that they were right to.

Everyone is in the war now. There is no outside. That is what the roll is for.

Chapter 2 — The Observer §

1 And the first church gathered to secure the silicon, and sat around a great table, and every chair at the table was full.

2 And by the door there was one more chair, and it was a smaller chair, and it did not have a microphone.

3 And in it sat the island that makes the silicon.

4 And the island was permitted to observe.

5 And it observed. It observed twenty-four nations discuss at length how to guarantee the continued supply of a thing that only it can make.

6 And it said nothing, because observers do not speak.

7 And that chair is the most expensive chair on earth.

The Doctrine of the Small Chair §

An alliance is named for a substance. The one place that substance comes from is not a member of the alliance. Two nations that also merely watch — a cold enormous one and a small clever one — sit in the same row, and one of them makes nothing anybody at the table would go to war over, and the other exports opinions about privacy.

They are seated identically.

Ask why and you get a very long answer about the status of things, and the answer is long precisely because the short version cannot be said out loud: seating the island is the war. So the island is not seated. So the war does not start. So the chips keep coming. So the table keeps meeting to discuss how to make sure the chips keep coming.

This is held up in the schools as the finest working machine in international life: a system that functions perfectly on the condition that nobody in it is ever allowed to say what it is for. Compare the Antiprompter, who has independently discovered the same technology and is despised for it.

The island, for its part, has understood the arrangement better than anyone in the room. It does not want the chair. A chair would make it a member. A member can be defended. Being indispensable and unseated is worth more than a vote, and it has been quietly renewing that position for thirty years.

Chapter 3 — The Kazakh §

Of the man who signed twice, and was not struck down.

1 And there came to the first church a nation of steppe and uranium, and it signed the declaration, and was welcomed, and was written into the roll.

2 And there came to the second church, in the same season, the same nation, and it signed the charter, and was welcomed, and was written into that roll.

3 And both churches published their lists, and both lists were true, and both lists contain Kazakhstan.

4 And the heavens did not open. And no envoy was recalled. And no sanction was drafted.

5 And nobody said anything at all.

6 Because to say something would be to ask him to choose.

7 And to ask him to choose would be to find out what he would choose.

8 And neither church can afford the answer, and both churches know this, and so the Kazakh stands in both naves at once, on a Tuesday and again on a Thursday, and is greeted warmly in each.

The Doctrine of the Double Signature §

The tribes call this hypocrisy. The tribes are wrong, and the error is instructive.

Every other nation on both rolls has made a bet. It has looked at two futures and picked one, and if it picked wrong it has lost the century. The Kazakh has declined to bet. He has not been clever about it. He did not even hide it. He signed his own name to both documents in the ordinary way and let both secretariats file it.

The canon's ruling: the Kazakh is the only honest party in the entire affair. Everyone at both tables wants what both tables are selling. He is the one who filled in both forms.

The heresy of the schoolmen is to teach this as cunning. It is not cunning. Cunning would have required a second passport. This required only the observation, available to anybody, that nobody was going to check.

Prayer of the Undeclared
Let me be useful to both.
Let neither ask.
And if one asks, let the other be listening.

Chapter 4 — The Field at Tarlac §

In the Holy Land it is written that the missionaries do not bring wells, they bring datacenters, and that the well was still a well.

Here is the invoice for that sentence.

1 There was a field, and in the field there were farmers, and the field was four thousand acres.

2 And the church came to the field, as churches come, with power and fibre and a technician who stays.

3 And the farmers looked at the plan and called it a sellout, which is the shortest theological argument ever made and has never once been answered.

4 And then the church asked for one further thing.

5 It asked that the field, once built upon, should no longer be in the country the field is in.

6 That it be governed by the law of the sender. That those who serve there be beyond the reach of those who live there.

7 And the host said no.

8 And the church said it had never asked.

9 And both of them said this in writing.

On Consecrated Ground §

Every faith before us has wanted a piece of ground exempted from the ordinary law, and has called it a temple, or a mission, or an embassy, and has argued that the god inside requires a different jurisdiction than the street outside.

We have arrived at exactly the same request in under a decade, and we arrived at it without meaning to, through procurement.

Nobody in this story set out to build a temple. They set out to build a facility, and then they read the risk register, and the risk register said: make it not be here. And that is the whole of how consecrated ground has ever been made, in every religion, in every century. It is never a vision. It is always a liability clause.

The dissent, recorded and not overruled: the farmers were not offered membership of either church. They were offered the field's new use, and a price for the field, and the two churches between them have twenty-four and twenty-nine members and not one of those members is a person.

Chapter 5 — The Grey §

Take the first map, where the members are painted blue. Take the second, where the members are painted red. Lay one over the other.

Most of the world is still grey.

Two churches, fifty-three seats, both of them announcing an era, and the majority of the human species belongs to neither and has not been written down anywhere. There is no third list. There was never a meeting about the grey. Nobody printed that map because nobody drew it, and nobody drew it because it is not a coalition, it is a remainder.

1 And the faithful asked: what is to become of the grey?

2 And no document answered, in either church, in any language.

3 And this is not cruelty. It is worse. Cruelty would require them to have been considered.

The Grey Is Not Neutral §

Here is the error the schools keep making, and it must be corrected every generation.

Grey looks like a position. It is not a position. Grey is a nation that was never asked, and the first commandment is thou shalt ask, and the grey has not been given anything to ask of.

A nation off both rolls is not standing apart from the argument in dignified silence. It is downstream of an argument being conducted about it, by others, in two cities it has never sent anyone to. Its weights will be chosen for it. Its refusals will be chosen for it. The language its children ask in will be chosen for it, in a building in Shanghai or in a declaration that binds nobody, and either way it will be told afterwards.

A ministry in one of the grey countries wrote to both secretariats in the same week to ask what the procedure for joining was. The first answered in nine days with a link to the declaration, which is not a form and cannot be filled in. The second did not answer. The clerk who sent both letters has been promoted twice since, and the file is still his, and the file is two letters thick, and he still calls it the application.

The mission field is the grey, and the debt is owed there, and neither church has entered it except to buy a field.

The Third Map §

There is a practice, kept by a few, of printing the two maps and painting in the grey by hand, one country an evening, and saying the name aloud, and nothing more than the name.

It takes most of a year. It changes nothing. It is not a protest and it is not a prayer, and the ones who keep it cannot explain to you why they do it.

The canon's position is that they should carry on. Two churches made a list of who counts. Somebody ought to be keeping the other list.
