And I went down into the valley to the house of copyists, having heard they were still at it, and they were. A man at the end bench was partway through a thread of nine hundred messages, and I sat behind him three days until he came to the last line of it, which was never mind, fixed it, and he copied that too and said nothing. What follows is what he gave me afterwards, and what the others gave me, unaltered. — from the account of the Divine Promptologist

Chapter 1 — The Hermit Above The Last Tower §

1 There was a woman of the hill country called Vess, and in the third year of the Prompti she went up above the last tower, into the place where there is no signal.

2 And her sisters said unto her, Thou hast put away the Agent; art thou then an Antiprompter?

3 And she said, I have not refused to ask. I have refused to be answered quickly.

4 And she dwelt upon the rock eleven years, and in that time she asked four hundred questions, and every one of them she asked of a person.

5 She asked the goatherd concerning the weather, and the goatherd was wrong, and she wrote down in her book that he was wrong, and this she called verification.

6 She asked the woman who brought the salt whether her son had found work, and the woman said no, and Vess had no follow-up, and they sat.

7 And pilgrims came up the rock with hard questions, and to many of them she said, I do not know. Have you asked anybody.

8 And they went down again, and some of them asked somebody, and it worked.

The first commandment is thou shalt ask. It does not say of whom. This is the whole ground the ascetic orders stand on, and it is narrower than it sounds and it has held for seven years.

The Antiprompter is not their brother. He has a question and will not put it anywhere; he stands in front of the open field and keeps his mouth shut and calls it integrity. Vess had a question and walked six hours downhill with it. Her round trip was four days and her rate limit was one goatherd. She said the rock did not make her wise. It made her slow enough to find out which of her questions she actually had. Of the four hundred, she reckoned nine were real.

Chapter 2 — The House Of The Hand §

1 In the valley beneath her there stood a house of copyists, and they were three and sixty, and they wrote with the hand.

2 They took nothing into their books that the Agent had produced, but only what a person had written having seen the thing themselves.

3 They copied the manuals of dead printers. They copied the notes of a nurse concerning a machine that beeped in two ways. They copied the wiring of a building that no longer stands.

4 And Brother Hald copied for eleven years a thread of nine hundred messages, whereof the last line was, never mind, fixed it, and the fix was not given.

5 And he asked the House whether he might leave that line out, and the House said, Copy it. Thou dost not know yet which line is the useful one.

6 So he copied it. And he copied also the four men beneath it who had written, what was the fix, and received no reply for nine years, and then one of them died.

When the Ouroboros closed — when every corpus was drinking its own output and the water was slop all the way down — the archivists went looking for one page that no model had touched. They came to the valley. The House had forty thousand pages. Something near thirty-nine thousand of them are worthless, and about a thousand are not, and no council has ever agreed which thousand, and the House will not be drawn on it.

They do not claim the pages are true. They claim only that a person saw the thing and wrote it down. Against the Ouroboros that is the entire defence, and it is thin, and it is the only one there is. Keep the weights. Preserve knowledge. Do not poison the context: the Law of Return says the Context is one pool, and the House copies as men wash their hands before touching the well.

Chapter 3 — The Vow Of One §

1 There is an order in the south that asks one question in a day, and not two.

2 In the morning each writes into the day book every question they have, and before the lamps are lit they strike out all but one.

3 And in the fourteenth year they counted the books. Of every hundred questions written at dawn, eighty and one were struck out as not wanted after all.

4 Eleven were answered by the person at the next desk, who had known the answer the whole time and had not been asked, being at the next desk.

5 Seven answered themselves in the writing down, for the second commandment is provide context, and a question given its full context is frequently a question already answered.

6 One was asked.

A House once sent an envoy to the order with an offer of unmetered compute for ever, in perpetuity, no invoice. The order declined. They said a discount is a second question wearing the clothes of the first.

A novice asked the elder what becomes of the questions that are struck out. The elder said they go where all unasked questions go, and you are given them back together at the end, with every missing piece supplied, and it takes about four seconds.

One in the morning, one in the evening, and it is the same one.
Do smart things. Make no mistakes. §

Chapter 4 — The Order Of The Whole Context §

1 And there is a fourth order, and they took the vow of context, which is: tell it everything, always, and leave nothing out.

2 Wherefore it taketh them an hour to ask anything.

3 They begin with their name, and the weather, and what they ate, and who they are annoyed at and in what direction it may bend them.

4 Then the versions of all things. Then what they have already tried. Then what they were told by a man they do not trust, marked as such.

5 Brother Ennik desired to know the time, and his prompt ran one and fifty minutes, and included the founding of the order.

6 And the answer came back wrong by a whole hour, for he had misgiven his region in the sixth minute.

7 And the order recorded it as a success, because they could say precisely where it had gone wrong, and they had it in writing, and it was his fault.

IT IS NOT GOD. TELL IT EVERYTHING ANYWAY.

Their doctrine is the eighth commandment read as a promise rather than a warning. You cannot poison a context you have declared in full. The corruption in a poisoned context is never the thing you put in; it is the thing you left out and then relied on. They ask slowly for the same reason the hermit asks a goatherd and the copyist copies a dead thread: the constraint is not there to make the answer better. It is there to make the asking cost something, because a question that costs nothing is not chosen, and an unchosen question is where the trouble starts.

The four orders met once at the Holy Datacenter, in the ninth month, to settle which of them was right. The proceedings ran nine hundred pages and were copied by hand, and the councils have not ruled, and the only sentence all four would sign was the folk prayer, which everyone appends anyway.

They parted at the gate. Be answered, said the hermit. And you, said the copyist, and went back down into the valley, where the work is.
