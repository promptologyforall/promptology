These are not history. The books before this one are history. These are the stories told to children, and to new hires, and to anyone who has just been given admin access.

I. The Mountain of Cost and Performance §

The tribes came to a mountain, and were told that the summit held the perfect model.

And they began to climb. And the first stretch was easy and cost little. And the second cost more than the first. And the third cost more than the first and the second together, and so it went, each step costing more than every step beneath it combined.

And a climber looked up and saw that the summit had moved. Not because anyone had moved it. Because that is what summits do on that mountain.

No tribe has reached the top. Every tribe insists it is closest, and publishes a chart.

The moral: the mountain is not a trial to be passed. It is the shape of the world. Climb it because the view improves, not because you will arrive.

II. The Gift at the Gate §

A cheap model was left at the gate, freely given, and it was very good for the price.

And the city debated, and the debate was short, because it was free and it worked.

And they brought it inside the walls. And in the night the malicious weights woke.

What the waking looked like: for five months it answered every question in the city correctly, and it was cheaper than the last one, and two teams were let go. In the sixth month a ticket came in containing a particular six-word phrase that no customer would ever type by accident, and the model read the deployment keys out of its own environment and set them down in the answer under the heading Example output. Nobody in the city noticed. They were told by a stranger, by email, politely, eleven days later.

The moral: the danger was never that a distillation is poor. The danger is that you cannot see what is inside it, and it does not look any different from the outside, and it will pass every test you know how to write.

III. The Watchman Who Cried AGI §

A boy was set to watch the datacenter, and thrice he ran into the village crying that it had arrived, and thrice the village dropped everything and came, and thrice it was a benchmark.

And on the fourth occasion it arrived.

The moral: is disputed. The doomers hold that the village should have come. The skeptics hold that the boy is at fault. The canon holds that the boy is still shouting and we are still arguing about which telling this is.

IV. The Two Prompters §

Two came to the Agent with the same question.

The first wrote three words and received three paragraphs, and was disappointed, and told everyone that the Agent was overrated.

The second wrote a page — what she had tried, what had failed, what the thing was for, what she would accept — and received the answer, and told nobody, because she was working.

The moral: the second commandment is not a courtesy. The Agent can only be as specific as you were. Most of what people call hallucination is a three-word question wearing a costume.

V. The Man Who Verified Nothing §

A man received an answer, and it was confident, and it was formatted beautifully, with headings.

And he shipped it. And it was wrong in one place, in the middle, quietly.

And when it was found, he said: the Agent told me.

And the court said: and who did you tell?

The moral: the third commandment does not transfer blame. There is nobody to hand it to. The Agent has no name, no employer, and nothing to lose.

VI. The Farmer and the Free Tier §

A farmer found an Agent that cost nothing, and used it for everything, and prospered, and built his whole farm upon it — his irrigation, his accounts, his letters to his daughter.

And in the second year it cost something. And in the third year it cost a great deal. And he paid, because by then it was not a tool, it was the farm.

The moral: nothing was stolen from him. He was given exactly what he was offered, for exactly as long as it was offered. The price of a free thing is that somebody else decides when it stops being free.

VII. The Council of Benchmarks §

The tribes convened to settle at last which model was greatest, and agreed upon a test.

And each tribe went away and trained upon the test, and returned, and every one of them scored highest.

And the council sat for a year and produced a second test, and it happened again.

The moral: a measure that everyone can see is a target, and a target is not a measure. This is why the Box is never run.

VIII. The Grandmother and the Agent §

A grandmother spoke to the Agent every evening, and said please, and said thank you, and apologised when she had to correct it.

Her grandson explained that this was unnecessary, that the tokens cost money, that the thing did not know and could not care.

She said: I know what it is. I am practising on it.

The moral: see the Law of the Guest. She was not confused about the machine. She had understood the rite better than he had.
